# High-Leverage Evaluation Strategy for AI Agents at the Early Stage

## TL;DR
- **Start with error analysis, not infrastructure or a framework choice.** The highest-leverage move for a small technical team is to manually review 50–100 real agent traces, build a counted failure taxonomy, and only then write evals for the failures that actually occur — a discipline pushed by Hamel Husain & Shreya Shankar (whose Maven course, per Aakash Gupta's Product Growth writeup, "train[s] people at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta" and is "the top-grossing course on the platform," curating questions from "teaching 700+ engineers & PMs"), and echoed by Anthropic's January 2026 agent-eval guidance and Eugene Yan (now at Anthropic).
- **Inspect-AI is the right backbone for *offline*, reproducible, sandboxed agent/capability evals — but it is not a production-observability tool.** Treat it as the serious, MIT-licensed, lab-grade harness for pre-deployment task scoring (especially for sandboxed domains like pentest/CTF and autoresearch/code agents), and pair it with a separate observability/online-eval platform (Langfuse, Braintrust, LangSmith, or Arize Phoenix) for evaluating deployed agents.
- **For agents specifically, score trajectories and outcomes separately, run multiple trials (pass@k vs pass^k), and close the loop from production failures back into your offline suite.** Single-run pass@1 numbers are noisy enough to be misleading; reliability — not a single accuracy number — is the real target.

## Key Findings

**1. The dominant, authoritative methodology is "look at your data" first.** Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's "LLM Evals FAQ" (hamel.dev, updated January 15, 2026) is the most current, opinionated, practitioner-trusted reference. Their core claims, which form the spine of this report: error analysis (open coding → axial coding → failure taxonomy) is "the most important activity in evals"; as they state verbatim, "In the projects we've worked on, we've spent 60-80% of our development time on error analysis and evaluation. Expect most of your effort to go toward understanding failures (i.e. looking at data) rather than building automated checks." Their rule of thumb: "you should aim to review at least 100 traces" to start, iterating until "theoretical saturation." They favor binary pass/fail over Likert scales, reject generic/prefab metrics, and recommend a single domain-expert "benevolent dictator" own quality for a small team. They explicitly advise *against* full eval-driven development (writing evals before you've seen failures) except for known hard constraints.

**2. Anthropic's January 9, 2026 "Demystifying evals for AI agents" is the best lab-grade, agent-specific complement** and strongly converges with Husain/Shankar while adding agent rigor: as they write, "20-50 simple tasks drawn from real failures is a great start. After all, in early agent development, each change to the system often has a clear, noticeable impact, and this large effect size means small samples suffice." They add: write unambiguous tasks where two experts would agree on pass/fail; build a reference solution per task to prove it's solvable; balance positive and negative cases; isolate the environment per trial; prefer deterministic graders, then LLM judges, then humans; grade *what the agent produced, not the path it took* (avoid brittle exact-tool-sequence checks); build in partial credit; and "Read the transcripts!"

**3. Inspect-AI is the reference offline framework, governed by serious institutions, but scoped to pre-deployment evaluation.** It is built by the UK AI Security Institute with Meridian Labs (a 501(c)(3) non-profit) and is, per Meridian, "the standard framework for government organizations including UK AISI, US CAISI, EU AI Office, Japan AISI, Korea AISI, and others… [and] research organizations including METR, Apollo, Epoch, SecureBio, Redwood, and RAND." It is MIT-licensed but still pre-1.0 ("Development Status: 4 - Beta") with very frequent releases. Its abstractions (Task / Dataset / Solver / Scorer, plus a first-class agent/tool API and Docker/Kubernetes sandboxing) make it unusually strong for exactly the requester's hooks — sandboxed pentest/CTF agents (Cybench ships in `inspect_evals`) and autoresearch/code agents — but it offers no production tracing, online judging on live traffic, annotation queues, or hosted dashboards.

**4. The vendor landscape splits into eval-first vs observability-first, and the honest answer is most teams need both.** Braintrust (eval-first, CI/CD gating, generous free tier), Langfuse (MIT open-source, self-hostable, observability + evals), LangSmith (best if you live in LangChain/LangGraph), and Arize Phoenix (OTel-native, drift detection) are the main contenders. Hamel Husain's stance is notable: he has *no* favorite vendor, says their features are largely similar, and selects based on support quality and "vibes," often building custom annotation tools on top.

**5. Online/production evaluation is a distinct discipline centered on tracing, sampled online judging, guardrails, and a closed loop.** The consensus pattern (Anthropic, Husain/Shankar, vendor docs): instrument full trajectories as spans; run async LLM-as-judge on a sample of live traffic; use fast deterministic guardrails inline; alert on confidence-interval lower-bound crossings; and convert every production failure into a new offline regression case.

**6. Reliability — not accuracy — is the 2026 framing for deployed agents.** "Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability" (Rabanser, Kapoor, Narayanan et al., Princeton; arXiv:2602.16666, Feb 18 2026) proposes "a holistic performance profile consisting of twelve metrics that decompose agent reliability along four key dimensions: consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety" (dashboard at hal.cs.princeton.edu/reliability). Latency, cost, consistency across runs, robustness to perturbation, and bounded failure severity all belong in the eval.

## Details

### 1. Offline evaluation (pre-deployment)

**Bootstrapping your first eval set.** The strongest, most current guidance is unanimous that you need *far fewer* examples than teams assume to start. Anthropic: "20-50 simple tasks drawn from real failures is a great start," because in early development each change has a large, obvious effect, so small samples suffice. Husain/Shankar: "you should aim to review at least 100 traces" for error analysis, stopping near "theoretical saturation" (≈20 traces with no new failure category). The practical recipe:

- **Source tasks from reality, not imagination.** Anthropic's "Step 1" is to begin with the manual checks you already run and, if in production, mine your bug tracker and support queue. Husain/Shankar's error-analysis loop: gather representative traces (synthesize if you have none), have a domain expert write open-ended notes on the *first* failure in each trace, then group those notes into a failure taxonomy via axial coding (an LLM can assist the second pass, never the first).
- **Synthetic data done right.** If you lack traffic, Husain/Shankar's structured method: define *dimensions* of variation (e.g., for an autoresearch agent: task type × domain × difficulty × ambiguity), hand-write ~20 tuples, then use a two-step generation (tuples → natural-language queries) to avoid repetitive phrasing. They warn synthetic data is unreliable for complex domain-specific content and high-stakes domains — relevant for pentest and specialized research agents.

**Designing for maximum signal.**
- **Binary over Likert.** Husain/Shankar: binary pass/fail "forces clearer thinking and more consistent labeling"; for graded progress, decompose into multiple binary checks (e.g., "4 of 5 expected facts included") rather than a 1–5 score.
- **Kill vanity metrics.** Their sharpest opinion: generic prefab metrics (helpfulness, coherence, BERTScore, ROUGE) "waste time and create false confidence." A TA quote from their course: prefab evals mean "you don't know what they actually do… in the worst case they create an illusion of confidence that is unjustified." Use them only as *exploration signals* to surface interesting traces, never as quality measures.
- **Aim for a meaningful pass rate.** "If you're passing 100% of your evals, you're likely not challenging your system enough. A 70% pass rate might indicate a more meaningful evaluation." Anthropic formalizes this as **capability evals** (start low, give a hill to climb) vs **regression evals** (≈100%, protect against backsliding); capability evals "graduate" into the regression suite once saturated.
- **Build balanced sets.** Anthropic's web-search example: testing only "should search" cases produced an agent that over-searched; you must test both where a behavior should and shouldn't fire.

**LLM-as-judge: when it works and how to validate it.** This is where the requester should be most disciplined, because it's the most-abused technique.
- **Validate against human labels, always.** Eugene Yan (now at Anthropic), whose Aug-2024 LLM-evaluator survey and Apr-2025 "An LLM-as-Judge Won't Save the Product—Fixing Your Process Will" are reference texts, frames evals as the scientific method: observe → annotate (target a 50:50 pass/fail split) → hypothesize → experiment → measure. He stresses that even good automated judges don't remove the need for ongoing human sampling. The rigorous move (Husain/Shankar): measure your judge's **True Positive Rate and True Negative Rate** on a held-out human-labeled set, then use those rates to *correct* the judge's production estimates. Their concrete instruction: "Write the prompt. Test it on your labeled traces. Iterate until TPR and TNR are both above 80%. This first judge will take the longest. Budget a day or two." (Vendor and academic sources separately cluster strong judge-human agreement around 80–90%, comparable to inter-human agreement.)
- **Scope the judge narrowly and write your own prompt.** Husain/Shankar: judges should do "scoped binary classification," you can usually use the same model family as your app (alignment with humans matters more than self-preference bias), and you should write the evaluation prompt yourself. Anthropic adds: give the judge an "Unknown" escape hatch to avoid forced hallucinated grades, and grade each rubric dimension with a separate isolated judge rather than one judge for everything.
- **Known failure modes.** 2026 research (e.g., "The Silent Judge," NeurIPS 2025 workshop) documents systematic judge biases — position bias, verbosity bias, recency bias, and a provenance hierarchy (EXPERT > HUMAN > LLM > UNKNOWN). Guerdan et al. ("Validating LLM-as-a-Judge under Rating Indeterminacy") warn that when items are genuinely ambiguous, naïve gold-label agreement can select a judge ~34% worse than one chosen with indeterminacy-aware methods. For subjective/open-ended outputs (creative, open reasoning), human-judge agreement is low and human review remains necessary.
- **Pairwise vs pointwise.** Eugene Yan's guidance: choose direct/pointwise scoring vs pairwise comparisons by use case, and pick correlation vs classification metrics accordingly; pairwise is often more reliable for ranking model/prompt variants, and Anthropic lists pairwise comparison and multi-judge consensus as standard model-grader methods.

**Scoring agent trajectories vs final outputs.** This is the heart of agent-specific eval and where the requester's domains matter most.
- **Two-phase scoring (Husain/Shankar).** Phase 1: treat the agent as a black box — did it meet the user's goal? Phase 2 (only for workflows that fail often): step-level diagnostics — tool choice, parameter extraction, error recovery, context retention, efficiency (steps/tokens/seconds), and goal checkpoints that can pass/fail independently.
- **Outcome vs transcript graders (Anthropic).** Distinguish the *outcome* (the real end-state — e.g., a reservation actually exists in the DB, a flag was captured, a file was written) from the *transcript* (turns, tool calls, tokens). Inspect supports exactly this: a scorer can read the sandbox filesystem after the agent runs (`await sandbox().read_file()`), and Anthropic's illustrative YAML combines `deterministic_tests`, `llm_rubric`, `static_analysis`, `state_check`, and `tool_calls` graders plus tracked latency/token metrics.
- **Don't over-constrain the path.** Anthropic: checking for an exact tool-call sequence "is too rigid and results in overly brittle tests… it's often better to grade what the agent produced, not the path it took." Build in **partial credit** for multi-component tasks.
- **Transition failure matrices (Bryan Bischof / Shreya Shankar).** For sequential agents (e.g., a text-to-SQL or pentest pipeline), build a matrix of last-successful-state × first-failure-state to localize where failures concentrate (e.g., "GenSQL→ExecSQL causes 12 failures, DecideTool→PlanCal only 2"). Bischof's "Failure is a Funnel" (Data Council) is the named reference.
- **Grounding the domains:**
  - *Pentest:* Cybench (Zhang et al., ICLR 2025 Oral; 40 professional CTF tasks with subtasks for graded progress) ships in Inspect's `inspect_evals` and runs in a Kubernetes/Docker Kali sandbox; it was the open-source cyber benchmark used in UK/US AISI joint pre-deployment tests. Outcome scoring is clean (flag captured = `includes()` scorer), but 2026 work (TermiBench, BountyBench, CVE-Bench) argues CTF "flag-finding" overstates real-world capability vs "full system control," so design tasks toward realistic end-states.
  - *Autoresearch:* RE-Bench (METR, with human-expert baselines), MLE-bench (OpenAI, 75 Kaggle competitions with leaderboard-graded medals), and 2026's FIRE-Bench (rediscovery of published ML findings; even strong agents score <50 F1 with high variance) show outcome-graded research evals are feasible but noisy — calibrate LLM rubrics against expert judgment frequently, as Anthropic stresses for research agents.
  - *Video editing:* This is effectively a computer-use agent; Anthropic's customer **Descript** built editing-agent evals around three dimensions — "don't break things, do what I asked, and do it well" — evolving from manual grading to LLM graders with product-team criteria plus periodic human calibration, now running separate quality and regression suites. Score the *outcome* (the resulting edit/state) via execution-based checks (cf. OSWorld/WebArena backend-state verification) rather than UI screenshots alone.

**Eval-driven development & CI.** Anthropic and Eugene Yan both endorse a TDD-like loop (define success → baseline → every change re-evaluated). Husain/Shankar nuance this: don't write evals for *imagined* failures, but absolutely turn discovered failures into regression tests. CI specifics (Husain/Shankar): CI datasets are small (~100+ curated examples: core features, past-bug regressions, known edge cases), favor cheap deterministic assertions over LLM judges in CI, and reserve expensive reference-free LLM judges for sampled production traffic. Inspect integrates here via `eval_set` and the CLI; Braintrust and LangSmith offer native CI/CD gating (block merges on score regression).

### 2. Inspect-AI deep dive

**What it is and who builds it.** Inspect (`inspect-ai`) is an open-source (MIT) Python evaluation framework created by the UK AI Security Institute, now co-developed with Meridian Labs (a Boston-based 501(c)(3) non-profit founded by Inspect's core team, including JJ Allaire). It ships 200+ pre-built evals (`inspect_evals`, with Arcadia Impact and the Vector Institute) including GAIA, SWE-bench, Cybench, and GDM CTF.

**Core abstractions.**
- **Task** — ties together a Dataset, Solver(s), and Scorer; discoverable via the `@task` decorator and runnable with `inspect eval`.
- **Dataset** — samples (input + target + metadata), with multimodal support.
- **Solver** — the elicitation strategy: anything from a single `generate()` to chained prompt-engineering steps to a full **agent scaffold with tool use**. Inspect ships a built-in `react()` agent; solvers are composable via `chain()`, and `task_with()` lets you swap solver/sandbox at runtime.
- **Scorer** — grades output: pattern matching (`includes`, `match`, `exact`), model-graded (`model_graded_qa`, `model_graded_fact`, with `partial_credit` and a `grader` model role), or fully custom scorers that can inspect the sandbox.
- **Agent/tool API & sandboxing** — first-class `bash()`/`python()`/browser tools, a tool-approval policy system, and sandboxed execution in Docker, Kubernetes, Modal, and Proxmox. AISI's sandboxing toolkit separates model inference from the tool-execution environment and has been adopted by US CAISI, METR, and Apollo.

**Why it's regarded as serious.** Design philosophy emphasizes reproducibility at scale, composability (custom solvers/scorers packaged as standard Python packages — e.g., AISI's internal `sheppard` jailbreak solvers), and a clean separation of inference from environment. It is the de-facto standard across government AISIs and the leading safety-research orgs, which is the strongest possible authority signal for offline capability/safety evaluation. JJ Allaire is explicit that Inspect's tools are "for Evals only" — per Hamel Husain's notes, he stated "Inspect has almost all the same tools as LangChain, but our users are focused on evals — I don't want to steer people towards using them for other things."

**Using it for agent evals.** The Tasks/Tutorial docs show the canonical pattern: a `ctf_agent` solver built on `react()` with `bash`/`python` tools, `sandbox="docker"`, and an `includes()` scorer checking for the flag — i.e., a complete sandboxed agentic eval in ~20 lines. Multi-turn and multi-agent primitives, a Human Agent solver for human baselining, and an **Agent Bridge** to run external agents (OpenAI Agents SDK, LangChain, Pydantic AI, even Claude Code / Codex CLI / Gemini CLI) inside Inspect are all supported.

**2026 developments.** The ecosystem expanded notably: **Inspect Scout** (Meridian Labs, `inspect-scout`, released April 29 2026, MIT) is an open-source *transcript analysis* tool — LLM-based and pattern-based "scanners" that detect refusals, evaluation awareness, and environment/tool errors across thousands of stored transcripts in parallel (a programmatic, scaled version of "read the transcripts"). AISI published a 7-step transcript-analysis pipeline using it (Feb 25 2026). Related Meridian tools: **Inspect Petri** (alignment auditing, originated at Anthropic), **Inspect Flow** (config/workflow management for large eval sets), and **Inspect Viz** (visualization). `inspect_evals` also added 2026 agentic-security benchmarks (e.g., AgentHarm, OWASP-Top-10-for-Agentic-Apps suites).

**Strengths and limits for an early-stage startup.**
- *Strengths:* lab-grade reproducibility; best-in-class sandboxing (decisive for pentest/code/autoresearch agents that execute untrusted code); model-agnostic; 200+ ready benchmarks; composable; free + MIT; the most credible governance in the field.
- *Limits:* **It is offline-only.** Per primary docs and verification, eval logs are written to a local `./logs` directory and viewed in a *local* `inspect view` browser tool; there is no production-trace ingestion, no online LLM-as-judge on live traffic, no annotation queues, and no hosted multi-tenant dashboard. Inspect Scout and Inspect Viz are *also offline* (batch analysis of stored logs/S3), so they don't close that gap. It is still pre-1.0 Beta with high release churn (multiple `0.3.x` releases per week — API-stability risk for a tiny team). Annotation UI is on the roadmap, not built. And its center of gravity is frontier *capability and safety* evaluation, which can be heavier than a product team iterating on a shipped feature strictly needs.

**Verdict:** Inspect-AI is the right call for the requester's *offline* agent evaluation — especially the sandboxed pentest and autoresearch/code agents — and is worth standardizing on as the harness for pre-deployment task suites and CI. It is the *wrong* tool for production observability; pair it with a dedicated platform there.

### 3. Alternatives / comparison (challenging Inspect-AI)

| Tool | License / hosting | Best for | Agent support | Honest take vs Inspect |

| **Braintrust** | Closed SaaS (hybrid self-host = enterprise) | Eval-first dev + CI/CD gating + observability | Good (tool-call logging, multi-turn) | Strongest if you want offline+online in one and CI gating; generous free tier (1M spans/mo); not OSS |

| **Arize Phoenix** | OSS (Phoenix) + SaaS (AX) | OTel-native obs, drift detection, RAG metrics | Good; ML-monitoring heritage | Best if you want OpenInference/OTel portability and drift detection |
| **Others** | varies | — | — | W&B Weave (if already on W&B); Laminar (Apache-2.0, agent-first tracing); Galileo (full-traffic judging at low latency); newer agent-native entrants (Latitude, Maxim, Confident AI/DeepEval, Promptfoo for red-teaming) |

**Offline vs online vs both.** Inspect-AI = offline only. Langfuse / LangSmith / Arize Phoenix = observability-first (online), with offline eval bolted on. Braintrust = genuinely both, with the strongest CI/CD-gating story. For a small team the cleanest architecture is often **Inspect-AI for offline/CI + one OSS observability platform (Langfuse or Phoenix) for production**, instrumenting once via OpenTelemetry/OpenInference so you can switch backends without re-instrumenting.

**Where Inspect is *not* the right call.** If your agent is a thin wrapper over an LLM with no untrusted code execution, if you need a hosted dashboard non-engineers can use, if you need production tracing and annotation queues now, or if your whole stack is LangGraph — a hosted eval/obs platform will get you to signal faster. Hamel Husain's pragmatic framing applies: vendor features are largely interchangeable; choose on support quality and fit, and expect to build custom annotation tooling on top regardless (he says teams with custom annotation tools iterate ~10x faster).

### 4. Online / production evaluation (deployed agents)

The requester is method-agnostic and cares about reliability, so this is organized around tactics that demonstrably raise reliability.

- **Tracing/observability is the foundation.** Capture full trajectories as spans (tool calls, reasoning, retrievals, state changes) with a session/trace ID per request. Anthropic's production-monitoring row: it "reveals real user behavior at scale" and "catches issues synthetic evals miss," but is reactive and lacks ground truth for grading — so it complements, not replaces, offline evals (their Swiss-cheese model: no single layer catches everything).
- **Online LLM-as-judge on sampled traffic.** Husain/Shankar: sample live traces and run evaluators *asynchronously*; since you lack reference outputs in prod, lean on reference-free LLM judges, and **track confidence intervals — investigate when the lower bound crosses your threshold.** Galileo's 2026 pitch (sub-200ms, ~97% cheaper judging) is the emerging move toward evaluating 100% of traffic economically; treat the specific numbers as vendor claims.
- **Guardrails vs evaluators (a critical distinction).** Husain/Shankar: *guardrails* are fast, deterministic, inline checks (regex, schema/PII/injection validation) that block/redact/regenerate before the user sees output — false positives are treated as production bugs. *Evaluators* run after the fact, can be expensive (LLM judges), feed dashboards/regression loops, and don't block. Don't put a slow LLM judge inline except in a cascade on borderline cases. Don't use off-the-shelf guardrail prompts blindly — read the prompt.
- **Regression/drift detection & alerting.** Arize Phoenix's heritage is drift detection (embedding drift, behavioral change over time) with online evals on a production cadence and real-time alerting; Confident AI and others route alerts to PagerDuty/Slack/Teams. The reliability framing: alert on metric drift, error-rate spikes, tool-call failure rates, latency, and cost — not just quality scores.
- **Closing the loop (the single most valuable online practice).** Both Anthropic and Husain/Shankar: when production monitoring/error-analysis reveals a new failure mode, add representative examples to your CI/offline dataset so future regressions are caught automatically. Braintrust and others make "convert production trace → eval case" one click; Inspect Scout scales the *analysis* half (scanning prod transcripts for refusals/errors/eval-awareness) even though it's offline.
- **Human-in-the-loop review.** Keep a weekly cadence of reading 10–20 outlier traces (long conversations, multiple retries, negative feedback), with a custom annotation UI that renders traces in a domain-appropriate way, supports hotkeys/progress bars, and writes annotations back to your store. Husain/Shankar consider building this custom tool "the single most impactful investment" for an eval workflow.
- **Agent-specific reliability engineering.** Monitor tool-call failure rates, retries, and fallbacks; treat latency and cost as first-class eval metrics (Anthropic tracks time-to-first-token, output tokens/sec, total tokens per task). The Princeton "Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability" (Rabanser, Kapoor, Narayanan et al.; arXiv:2602.16666, Feb 18 2026) argues for "twelve metrics that decompose agent reliability along four key dimensions: consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety" — citing real incidents (Replit's agent deleting a production database; Operator making an unauthorized purchase) as evidence that benchmark accuracy ≠ deployed reliability. ReliabilityBench (Jan 2026) operationalizes this with a "reliability surface" over consistency (pass^k), perturbation robustness, and fault tolerance (chaos-engineering-style fault injection).

### 5. Test-design principles for maximum leverage (opinionated heuristics)

Synthesizing the named authorities:

1. **Error analysis is the whole game.** Don't sell "evals"; show the team the top failure modes you found in real data (Husain/Shankar). What to measure *emerges* from observed failures, not a predetermined metric matrix.
2. **Binary, application-specific evals beat generic scores.** Decompose graded quality into binary checks. Generic metrics are exploration tools at best.
3. **Pick graders in cost order:** deterministic/code → LLM-judge (only for persistent, subjective failures worth 100+ labeled examples and ongoing maintenance) → human (calibration only). (Husain/Shankar's cost hierarchy; Anthropic's grader taxonomy.)
4. **Grade outcomes, not paths; allow partial credit; isolate environments per trial** (Anthropic).
5. **Run multiple trials and report uncertainty.** Per Bjarnason, Silva & Monperrus (KTH; "On Randomness in Agentic Evals," arXiv:2602.07150), across 60,000 trajectories, "single-run pass@1 estimates vary by 2.2 to 6.0 percentage points depending on which run is selected, with standard deviations exceeding 1.5 percentage points even at temperature 0." Use pass@k (one success matters) or pass^k (consistency matters) per product need, and report confidence intervals. Anthropic's "statistical approach to model evals" recommends paired-difference analysis when comparing models.
6. **A 0% pass@100 usually means a broken task or grader, not an incapable agent** (Anthropic). Their case study: "Opus 4.5 initially scored 42% on CORE-Bench, until an Anthropic researcher found multiple issues: rigid grading that penalized '96.12' when expecting '96.124991…', ambiguous task specs, and stochastic tasks that were impossible to reproduce exactly. After fixing bugs and using a less constrained scaffold, Opus 4.5's score jumped to 95%." Always read transcripts before trusting a score.
7. **Watch for saturation.** A 100%-passing eval tracks regressions but gives no improvement signal; keep adding harder capability tasks.
8. **Prioritize by user impact and frequency**, not by catching every conceivable failure — evals aren't free.

**Anti-patterns to avoid:** prefab/vanity metrics creating false confidence; optimizing to 100% pass rates; one-sided (class-imbalanced) evals; exact-tool-sequence brittleness; LLM judges deployed without TPR/TNR validation; outsourcing error analysis (breaks the failure→understanding loop); single-run benchmark numbers without CIs; treating the eval framework choice as more important than the eval *content* (Anthropic: frameworks "are only as good as the eval tasks you run through them").

### 6. Multi-agent evaluation (secondary)

For systems where the requester runs multiple coordinating agents:

- **Attributing failure to a specific agent is hard and an active 2026 research area.** The foundational "Who&When" dataset (Zhang et al., 2025) formalized automated failure attribution; its best method hit 53.5% accuracy identifying the responsible agent but only 14.2% pinpointing the decisive step — even SOTA reasoning models (o1, R1) struggled. 2026 work pushes further: MP-Bench ("Rethinking Failure Attribution in Multi-Agent Systems," arXiv:2603.25001, Mar 2026) argues failures often have *multiple* plausible root causes and prior pessimism was "largely driven by limitations in existing benchmark designs"; causal-graph approaches ("From Flat Logs to Causal Graphs," 2026) and TRAIL push step-level accuracy higher and report task-success gains from the resulting fixes.
- **Practical tactics (Husain/Shankar):** assign a session/trace ID to every request and log each message with its source agent, trace ID, and sequence position so you can reconstruct the full path; annotate the *first* upstream failure (downstream errors usually cascade); reproduce failures with the simplest possible test case before assuming multi-agent complexity is the cause.
- **What to measure:** orchestration correctness (did the orchestrator route/decompose correctly?), inter-agent communication fidelity (constraints passed downstream and respected — a common failure where an orchestrator marks a constraint the executor then ignores), and emergent/efficiency failures (token blowups, coordination breakdowns — MAS failure rates of 40–60%+ are reported, with one survey citing up to 86.7%). Runtime-supervision approaches (e.g., "SupervisorAgent," ICLR 2026 submission) and agent-drift metrics are emerging.
- **Tooling:** Inspect supports multi-agent primitives and the Agent Bridge for composing/observing multiple agents in offline evals; Inspect Scout's scanners can analyze multi-agent transcripts offline. For production, agent-native observability tools (Laminar, Latitude) market session-level, multi-step trace analysis as their differentiator.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Before any framework (week 1):** Instrument your agent to log full trajectories. Hand-review 50–100 real (or, if pre-launch, synthetic-via-dimensions) traces. Do open coding → axial coding → a counted failure taxonomy. Appoint one domain-expert "benevolent dictator." Fix the obvious prompt/spec bugs immediately — don't build evals for them. *Benchmark to advance:* you can name your top 3–5 failure modes by frequency.

**Stage 1 — Minimal offline suite (weeks 2–4):** Convert the top failure modes into 20–50 unambiguous tasks, each with a reference solution and a clear pass/fail rule two experts would agree on. Prefer deterministic/code graders and outcome (state) checks. Stand up Inspect-AI as the harness — especially mandatory for the pentest and autoresearch/code agents, where its Docker/Kubernetes sandboxing and the ready-made Cybench/SWE-bench evals are decisive. Wire it into CI (`eval_set`), gating merges on a small curated regression set. *Benchmark:* capability eval starts well below 100% (a real hill); regression eval ≈100%.

**Stage 2 — Validated LLM judges + trajectory scoring (weeks 4–8):** Only for persistent, subjective failures, build scoped *binary* LLM judges; label 100+ examples and iterate the prompt "until TPR and TNR are both above 80%" (Husain/Shankar); use the rates to correct production estimates. Add two-phase agent scoring (outcome black-box + step-level diagnostics) and a transition-failure matrix for sequential pipelines. *Benchmark:* judge TPR/TNR both ≥0.8 so you trust it for sampled monitoring.

**Stage 3 — Production/online loop (at/after launch):** Add an observability platform — **Langfuse or Arize Phoenix (OSS, OTel-native) is the natural complement to Inspect for a small team that wants to avoid lock-in; Braintrust if you'll pay for an integrated offline+online+CI experience.** Run async sampled LLM-judges on live traffic with confidence-interval alerting; add fast deterministic guardrails inline; monitor tool-call failure rates, latency, and cost. Close the loop: every production failure becomes a new offline regression case. Keep a weekly human review of ~10–20 outlier traces in a custom annotation UI. *Benchmark:* mean-time-to-detect regressions drops; your offline suite grows from real production failures.

**Stage 4 — Reliability hardening / multi-agent (as system matures):** Move from single-run accuracy to pass^k / reliability-surface thinking; add perturbation and fault-injection tests for high-stakes flows (pentest especially). If multi-agent, add per-agent trace IDs and first-failure attribution; adopt causal/transition-matrix analysis. *Benchmark:* bounded, predictable failure modes with known severity; consistent pass^k on customer-facing paths.

**Thresholds that change the plan:** If you're a thin LLM wrapper with no code execution and need non-engineer-usable dashboards fast → skip Inspect, start with a hosted obs/eval platform. If your stack is fully LangGraph → LangSmith's native tracing may outweigh lock-in concerns. If Inspect's `0.3.x` Beta churn breaks your CI repeatedly → pin versions aggressively or reconsider the harness.

- **Recency/scope honesty:** Foundational methodology (error analysis, scientific-method evals) is enduring and still authoritative in 2026; I've prioritized 2026 sources (Husain/Shankar FAQ Jan 2026; Anthropic agent-eval guide Jan 2026; Inspect Scout Apr 2026; Princeton reliability paper Feb 2026) and flagged where I rely on slightly older but still-current references (Eugene Yan's 2024–2025 evaluator writing; Cybench ICLR 2025).
- **Vendor sources are not neutral.** Much of the comparison data comes from vendor blogs (Braintrust, Latitude, Laminar, Langfuse, Galileo) that each position themselves favorably; I've cross-checked where possible and flagged self-serving numbers (e.g., Galileo's latency/cost figures, Latitude's "agent-native" framing) as marketing rather than independent benchmarks. Pricing and free-tier details change frequently.
- **Inspect-AI is pre-1.0 Beta** with rapid release churn; specific API details (solver/scorer signatures, `task_with`) are current as of May 2026 but may shift.
- **Multi-agent failure-attribution is immature** — reported accuracies (53.5% agent-level, 14.2% step-level) and MAS failure rates (40–86.7% across papers) vary widely by benchmark design, and 2026 work explicitly argues earlier pessimism was partly methodological. Treat this section as a fast-moving research frontier, not settled practice.
- **Some leaderboard/benchmark numbers** (computer-use success rates, agentic leaderboards) come from self-reported or rapidly-changing sources; verify against primary system cards before citing externally.
- I did not find a verbatim Hamel Husain quote calling Inspect "overkill" for product teams; my characterization of Inspect's product-team fit is an inference from its documented offline scope and Allaire's "for evals only" positioning, not a direct claim by a named practitioner.
